CASE STUDY · RENARD BAKERY · BRUSSELS

From a one-day audit to the bakery's command center.

Renard Bakery was about to open a second store. Every number they needed was sitting in the register system, and nobody could pull it out in a form they could read. Less than five months later, management opens one app on their phones every day. It shows yesterday's sales, the forecast for the week ahead, and what to bake more of tomorrow. Here's how we got there, from the first audit day to today.

01

Where things stood

Renard Bakery is an artisan bakery in Brussels. Everything is baked in one central kitchen. In June 2026 they had one store in Ixelles and a second one in Uccle set to open two months later. They also deliver to businesses, sell through an online store, and take orders on Deliveroo. They're open seven days a week.

The bread was great and the business was growing. The trouble was that it all ran on what a few people kept in their heads. How much to bake came down to experience and gut feel. Nobody tracked what was left at closing time. The register system had all the data, but getting a simple answer out of it, like what a given day brought in, meant digging through reports that take training to read.

That works with one store. Add a second store, four sales channels and a staff that keeps turning over, and it stops working.

02

The audit day

On June 24 I spent a day in their production kitchen. I didn't bring slides. I watched and asked questions. How do orders come in? Who decides what gets baked? Where does the time go? Which tools are they already paying for, and what do those cost?

A week later they had my report. It named five areas where time and money were leaking:

  • Forecasting and waste: production runs on gut feel, and nobody measures leftovers.
  • Financial visibility: the data is all in the register system, but there's no daily picture.
  • Staff scheduling: a large, rotating crew gets scheduled through scattered messages.
  • Customer communication: reviews and newsletters are handled by hand, when they're handled at all.
  • Know-how: every new hire gets the same walkthrough from scratch.

The same thing kept coming up: what the business knew lived in people's heads instead of in a system. Every proposal I made came back to getting that knowledge out, so the chaos doesn't grow along with the company.

The report closed with ten priorities in order, a review of their tools and subscriptions, and three proposals: a light starter package with quick wins, a full ecosystem, and everything combined in one app.

03

What they chose: fewer things, done deeper

What happened next is the part I still think about. They passed on the biggest package, and they passed on the quick wins too. They cut everything that was nice to have and kept the two heaviest pieces: financial visibility and forecasting, in one app on their phones, with training included so the team would really use it.

I think that was a smart call. An audit puts the whole menu in front of you. Then you have to pick what comes first and let the rest wait.

We had a signed agreement on July 13 and held the kickoff four days later.

04

What we built.

We built them their own platform instead of another dashboard bolted onto the register system. It has a login, a database that updates automatically from their source systems, and a calculation layer that works out every number before it shows up on a screen. Register sales, Deliveroo, weather and school vacations all feed into it.

Real screens, made-up numbers

01

The daily overview

Sales for yesterday, the past week and the month, by store or combined. You get the comparison with last year, the pattern by day of the week, the customer count and the average ticket. A short plain-language briefing sits at the top: what's fine, what to keep an eye on, what needs action.

02

Each channel on its own

In-store, Deliveroo, B2B and the online store, side by side. For Deliveroo you see what's left after commission as well as the sales. Since October the orders flow in directly through a Deliveroo integration, so nobody uploads files anymore.

03

What sells and what doesn't

The best and worst sellers for each store. What's climbing, and what has dropped three periods in a row. Which small part of the product line brings in half the sales. And which product group peaks at which hour of the day.

04

The forecast

Seven days ahead, with expected sales for each day plus a low and a high estimate. The model looks at the day of the week, recent sales levels, school vacations and the weather. It also grades itself daily: next to each prediction you see what really happened. Tested against more than 400 days of history, it was off by about 8 percent of daily sales on average.

05

From numbers to action

This is the screen where numbers turn into decisions. It shows which products sell out too early on busy days, which means lost sales you won't see anywhere else. It flags products that have been sliding for months and might be worth dropping. And it points out which weekday is slow enough for a top product to deserve a promotion.

06

AI that reads the numbers with you

Claude reads the numbers too. Each day it writes a short piece of advice on what to do today and why, based on the platform's own data.

07

On the phone, in two languages

The platform sits on their home screens like any other app. It runs in Dutch and French because that's how the team works. Anyone who needs an overview for a meeting can put together a report and save it as a PDF.

Three rules I don't bend

No number without a source.

If we can't back a number up, we don't show it. The screen says it's unavailable and gives the reason. I'd rather show an empty box than a made-up margin.

An untested forecast is just an opinion.

Every forecast comes from a model that was graded against the past first, and the platform shows its track record.

No customer data.

The database holds only aggregated sales figures. There are no names and no addresses. It's hosted in the EU, every user has a personal login with two-step sign-in, and a data processing agreement is in place.

05

How we worked together

From week one the team had their own space in my hub. It held the project broken into phases, tasks for each person with a deadline, the meeting notes, and a place to leave feedback. We had a short status meeting every Thursday. They also got a shared Claude Team workspace so they could start using AI themselves.

It wasn't a straight line. We were in the middle of summer vacation season, and the second store opened right in the thick of it. The first version went online on August 18, six days before the Uccle opening. After that came the real work: sitting down together and going through it screen by screen. What's missing? What's hard to read? What can go?

Their feedback was specific. Make it more businesslike. Use less color. Put today's sales on the first screen. Give the worst sellers their own list. Feedback like that makes a platform better, and it's why I never ship something the people using it haven't tried first.

07

And it keeps growing

They've been on a retainer since November 1. I keep the platform running, there's a check each morning that the numbers came in, and once a month I verify that the forecast still holds up. Small changes are included. Anything new gets a price first, so there are no surprises.

A next phase is planned for early 2027. The idea is to log each evening what was left over and what ran short, so the platform can turn that into a production list. We'd add margin per product. There'd be a chat where the team can ask whatever they want to know. And there'd be roles, so the stores can work in it along with management.

One more thing: the platform belongs to them. If they ever stop working with me, I move everything to their own accounts. We put that in writing on day one.
